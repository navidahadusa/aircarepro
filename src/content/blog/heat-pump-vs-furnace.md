<h2>How they differ</h2><p>A gas furnace burns natural gas to create heat. A heat pump uses electricity to move heat from outdoor air into your home, and reverses to provide cooling in summer.</p><h2>Cold-weather performance</h2><p>Furnaces produce the same hot air regardless of outdoor temperature. Heat pumps lose capacity as it gets colder, though cold-climate models perform much better than older ones.</p><h2>Comfort</h2><p>Furnace air feels hotter coming out of vents. Heat pumps deliver gentler, steadier warmth over longer cycles.</p><h2>Operating cost</h2><p>Cost depends on local gas and electricity prices, your home&#x27;s efficiency and how the system is set up. In mild weather heat pumps are often very economical.</p><h2>Dual-fuel: the best of both</h2><p>A dual-fuel system uses the heat pump for most of the year and switches to the furnace in deep cold. It&#x27;s a popular choice for Chicago homes with gas service.</p><h2>Making the decision</h2><p>We&#x27;ll look at your home, current equipment and goals and recommend the option that makes the most sense.</p><h2>Installation considerations</h2><p>A furnace connects to gas, venting and your ductwork. A heat pump needs an outdoor unit, refrigerant lines and enough electrical capacity. If you already have central air, replacing it with a heat pump is a natural upgrade. If you have no ductwork, ductless heat pumps are an option.</p><h2>Lifespan</h2><p>Furnaces commonly last 15 to 20 years. Heat pumps, which run year-round for both heating and cooling, often last somewhat less. Regular maintenance helps both last longer.</p><h2>Environmental considerations</h2><p>Heat pumps use electricity instead of burning gas in your home, which can reduce on-site emissions. Many homeowners consider this along with cost and comfort when choosing.</p><h2>Dual-fuel switchover</h2><p>In a dual-fuel system, the thermostat switches from the heat pump to the furnace at a set outdoor temperature. The ideal setting depends on equipment efficiency and energy prices. We set it up to balance comfort and cost.</p><h2>Getting expert advice</h2><p>Every home is different. We&#x27;ll look at your insulation, ductwork, current equipment and goals, then explain your options in plain language so you can choose with confidence.</p><h2>Maintenance needs</h2><p>Furnaces need yearly heating tune-ups, while heat pumps need attention in both seasons because they run year-round. Both benefit from regular filter changes. Dual-fuel systems need both the furnace and heat pump maintained.</p><h2>Noise</h2><p>Modern variable-speed heat pumps and furnaces are both quiet. Outdoor heat pump units run in winter as well as summer, so placement away from bedroom windows is worth considering.</p><h2>Common misconceptions</h2><p>Many people believe heat pumps don&#x27;t work in cold climates. Older ones struggled, but today&#x27;s cold-climate models and dual-fuel setups work well in the Chicago area when properly designed and installed.</p><h2>Next steps</h2><p>If you&#x27;re replacing an air conditioner or furnace, ask us to include a heat pump or dual-fuel option in your estimate so you can compare.</p><h2>Questions to ask yourself</h2><p>How long do you plan to stay in your home? Do you have existing ductwork in good condition? Is your air conditioner also aging? How important are energy bills, upfront cost and environmental impact to you? Do you want to keep gas service? Your answers help determine whether a furnace, heat pump or dual-fuel system makes the most sense. We&#x27;ll walk through these questions with you during a free estimate and recommend the option that fits your priorities.</p><h2>Comfort in the coldest weeks</h2><p>In late January, when temperatures can stay below zero for days, a gas furnace or dual-fuel backup ensures strong, reliable heat. That&#x27;s why dual-fuel remains so popular in the Chicago area.</p><h2>What we typically recommend in Chicago</h2><p>For homes with gas service, existing ductwork and an aging air conditioner, a dual-fuel system is often the sweet spot: efficient heat pump operation most of the year, strong furnace heat in deep cold and modern air conditioning in summer. For homes without gas, or homeowners focused on reducing gas use, a cold-climate heat pump with electric backup can work well, especially in well-insulated homes. For homes where the furnace is newer and the AC is fine, there&#x27;s often no reason to change anything yet.</p><h2>The bottom line</h2><p>Both furnaces and heat pumps can keep a Chicago home comfortable. The right choice depends on your home and priorities, and we&#x27;re happy to help you compare.</p>
