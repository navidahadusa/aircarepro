Walk into the basement of a Chicago greystone or two-flat and there's a good chance you'll find a steam boiler. Bungalows, on the other hand, are more often heated by hot water, and newer homes almost always are. Both feed radiators, and from upstairs they can look the same. They aren't. Steam and hot-water systems work differently, fail differently and need different care, so it's worth knowing which one you have before the first no-heat night.

Here's how to tell in about five minutes, without tools.

## 1. Look for a gauge glass on the boiler

A **steam boiler** has a small vertical glass tube on its side, usually with a valve at the top and bottom. That's the gauge glass (also called a sight glass), and you should see water partway up it. Water level matters on steam because the boiler is literally boiling water away into steam.

A **hot-water boiler** has no gauge glass. Its water fills the whole system, so there's no level to watch.

## 2. Read the gauge

Both kinds have a gauge, but the numbers differ:

- **Steam:** a pressure gauge that reads very low, often on a small scale, plus a gray control box called a pressuretrol mounted on a looped pipe (a "pigtail"). Residential steam runs at very low pressure.
- **Hot water:** a round gauge that shows pressure and often temperature together (a "tridicator"). When the system is cold it shows noticeably higher pressure than a steam boiler ever should. For what a normal reading looks like, see our [boiler pressure guide](/boiler-pressure-guide/).

## 3. Follow the pipes

A **hot-water system** has two parts a steam system doesn't:

- A **circulator pump**: a small motor, often painted red, green or gray, set into the piping near the boiler.
- An **expansion tank**: either a small, round tank hanging from the pipes or a long cylinder strapped up near the basement ceiling.

Many hot-water systems also have **zone valves**, small boxes on the pipes with wires running to them, one per thermostat.

A **steam system** has no pump moving water to the radiators. Instead you'll see large-diameter pipes rising from the top of the boiler and long pipes (mains) running across the basement ceiling, usually with one or two vents at the far ends.

## 4. Check a radiator

Go upstairs and look closely at one of the cast-iron radiators:

- **A metal vent sticking out of the side, near the top, shaped like a small bullet or cylinder:** steam. That vent lets air out so steam can come in, and it often hisses as the system heats up.
- **One pipe connected at the bottom:** almost always one-pipe steam, the most common type in older Chicago buildings.
- **A small valve near the top that takes a square key or a screwdriver:** hot water. That's the bleeder you open to let trapped air out ([air in a boiler system](/air-in-boiler-system/) explains when and how).
- **Two pipes, one at each end:** hot water, or occasionally two-pipe steam. The vent and the boiler checks above settle it.

Baseboard heat (long, low metal fins along the walls) and radiant floors are always hot water.

## 5. Listen

Steam has a sound: hissing vents as the system starts, the occasional knock or clank as steam meets cooler pipe, and radiators that heat up all at once. Hot water is quieter: a gentle hum from the pump and radiators that warm up gradually. Loud banging on a steam system isn't normal, and it usually means water is pooling where it shouldn't; see [banging steam pipes](/steam-heat-banging-pipes/).

## Why the type matters

**Maintenance is different.** A steam boiler needs its water level watched, its low-water cutoff tested and its vents kept working. A hot-water system needs the right pressure, a healthy expansion tank and a working circulator.

**Problems look different.** On steam, cold radiators usually point to vents, water level or pipe pitch. On hot water, they usually point to air, a failed pump or a stuck zone valve. Our guide to a [boiler not heating](/boiler-not-heating/) walks through both.

**Some home fixes don't carry over.** Never "bleed" a steam radiator: there's no bleeder, and opening the vent won't help. And never add water to a hot steam boiler with an empty gauge glass. Turn it off and let it cool first.

**Replacement options are different.** High-efficiency condensing boilers only work on hot-water systems. Steam boilers are replaced with steam boilers, unless you convert the whole system, which means looking at every radiator and pipe.

## Two-flats with one boiler

Many Chicago two-flats share one boiler between both units. With hot water, the building can often be zoned so each floor gets its own thermostat. With one-pipe steam, the whole building heats together, and comfort depends on balancing the radiator vents floor by floor. If you own a two-flat and the top floor bakes while the garden unit stays cold, the system type is the first thing we look at.
