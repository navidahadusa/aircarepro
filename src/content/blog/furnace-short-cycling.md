<h2>What short cycling looks like</h2><p>A furnace is short cycling when it runs for only a few minutes, shuts off and starts again soon after, over and over. Sometimes the burners light and go out within seconds. Other times the furnace heats briefly and then stops before the house reaches temperature. Either way, short cycling makes temperatures swing, raises gas bills and puts extra wear on igniters, motors and controls.</p><h2>Overheating and the high-limit switch</h2><p>The most common cause is overheating. When airflow is restricted by a dirty filter, closed vents, a dirty blower wheel or undersized ductwork, heat builds up inside the furnace. The high-limit switch shuts off the burners to protect the heat exchanger, the blower cools things down and the cycle repeats. Fixing the airflow problem fixes the cycling.</p><h2>Dirty flame sensor</h2><p>If the burners light and then shut off within a few seconds, the flame sensor may be dirty. The control board can&#x27;t prove the flame, so it closes the gas valve as a safety measure and tries again. Cleaning the sensor is a quick fix during a service call or tune-up.</p><h2>An oversized furnace</h2><p>Many older Chicago homes have furnaces that are much larger than the home needs, especially after new windows and insulation. An oversized furnace heats the house so quickly that it reaches the set temperature in minutes and shuts off, then starts again as soon as the temperature drops. The long-term fix is a properly sized furnace, ideally a two-stage or modulating model.</p><h2>Thermostat location and settings</h2><p>A thermostat near a heat register, in direct sunlight or next to a drafty door can misread the room temperature. Some thermostats also have cycle-rate settings that can be adjusted. Moving the thermostat or correcting its settings can solve the problem.</p><h2>Venting and pressure switch issues</h2><p>On high-efficiency furnaces, a partially blocked vent, a waterlogged condensate trap or a marginal pressure switch can cause the furnace to start, lose the pressure signal and shut down. These issues need testing with a manometer to diagnose properly.</p><h2>How short cycling affects your bills</h2><p>Every time a furnace starts, it uses extra energy to light the burners, run the inducer and warm up the heat exchanger before useful heat reaches your rooms. A furnace that short cycles spends a larger share of its run time in that inefficient start-up phase. The result is higher gas bills, more electrical use and noticeably less comfort, with rooms that swing between too warm and too cool.</p><h2>How short cycling affects equipment life</h2><p>Igniters, relays, inducer motors and gas valves all have limited lifespans measured in cycles. A furnace that starts three times as often as it should wears these parts out much faster. Overheating also stresses the heat exchanger, which can lead to cracks over time. Fixing short cycling early protects your furnace and can add years to its life.</p><h2>Ductwork and return air</h2><p>Many Chicago homes have ductwork that was installed decades ago for a different furnace or added after the home was built. Undersized returns are especially common. When the blower can&#x27;t pull in enough air, the furnace overheats and trips its limit switch. Adding a return or enlarging an existing one can stop short cycling and make the system quieter at the same time.</p><h2>When a new furnace is the answer</h2><p>If your furnace is oversized, older and short cycling constantly, replacing it with a properly sized two-stage or modulating furnace usually solves the problem for good. These furnaces run at lower output most of the time, producing longer, gentler cycles and more even temperatures. We&#x27;ll size the new furnace with a load calculation and look at ductwork at the same time.</p><h2>Check your filter first</h2><p>Before calling, check and replace your filter and make sure all supply and return vents are open. If short cycling stops, airflow was the problem. If it continues, call for service so a technician can test the flame sensor, limit switch, pressure switch and thermostat.</p><h2>Thermostat placement matters</h2><p>A thermostat placed near a supply register, kitchen, sunny window or exterior door reads temperatures that don&#x27;t reflect the rest of the house. Moving it to an interior wall away from heat sources can stop short cycling and improve comfort throughout the home.</p><h2>The bottom line</h2><p>Short cycling is a symptom, not a diagnosis. The cause could be as simple as a dirty filter or as involved as an oversized furnace or undersized ductwork. A proper diagnosis finds the real reason, and fixing it improves comfort, lowers bills and protects your furnace from unnecessary wear.</p>
