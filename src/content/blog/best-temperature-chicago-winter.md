<h2>A good starting point</h2><p>Many people are comfortable around 68 to 70 degrees while home and awake. Lower it a few degrees at night or when you&#x27;re away to save on gas.</p><h2>Don&#x27;t turn it off completely</h2><p>On very cold days, avoid big setbacks in older homes and never turn the heat off when you travel. Frozen pipes are far more expensive than a few degrees of heat.</p><h2>Humidity changes how warm you feel</h2><p>Dry winter air feels colder. Keeping indoor humidity around 30 to 40 percent makes 68 feel more comfortable.</p><h2>Let a smart thermostat help</h2><p>Schedules and remote control make it easy to save without thinking about it. Just make sure it&#x27;s configured correctly for your system.</p><h2>Setbacks and recovery</h2><p>Lowering the temperature 7 to 10 degrees for eight hours a day can reduce heating costs. With heat pumps, smaller setbacks are better to avoid triggering backup heat during recovery.</p><h2>Keep doors and vents open</h2><p>Closing vents in unused rooms can increase pressure in the ductwork and reduce efficiency. It&#x27;s usually better to keep vents open and use zoning if you want different temperatures in different areas.</p><h2>Seal drafts</h2><p>Weatherstripping doors, sealing gaps around windows and adding attic insulation keep warm air inside, letting you stay comfortable at a lower setting.</p><h2>Sleeping temperature</h2><p>Many people sleep best in a cooler room, somewhere in the mid to upper 60s. Lowering the thermostat at night saves energy and can improve sleep quality.</p><h2>Homes with infants or older adults</h2><p>Babies, elderly family members and anyone with certain health conditions may need warmer, steadier temperatures. Comfort and health come before savings.</p><h2>What about while traveling?</h2><p>When you leave for a winter trip, set the thermostat around 55 to 60 degrees to protect pipes, especially pipes on outside walls. A smart thermostat lets you check on your home and get alerts if the temperature drops.</p><h2>Smart thermostat tips</h2><p>Use schedules for weekdays and weekends, enable geofencing so the thermostat knows when you&#x27;re away and set up alerts for unusual temperatures. Small, consistent setbacks are the easiest way to save.</p><h2>Comfort beyond the thermostat</h2><p>Balanced humidity, sealed drafts, well-placed furniture away from vents and regular furnace maintenance all help you stay comfortable at a lower setting.</p><h2>Older homes need special care</h2><p>Many Chicago homes are a century old, with plaster walls, original windows and pipes running through exterior walls. These homes can lose heat quickly and have cold spots where pipes are at risk during deep freezes. In older homes, avoid deep setbacks during sub-zero weather, keep cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls open and consider a modest overnight setback rather than a large one. If certain rooms are always cold, that&#x27;s a sign of a duct, radiator or insulation issue worth addressing.</p><h2>Boiler homes and thermostat settings</h2><p>Radiator systems respond slowly. Because cast-iron radiators and the water inside them take time to heat up and cool down, large setbacks can leave a boiler-heated home chilly for hours in the morning. Smaller setbacks and steady temperatures usually work better with boilers. Some modern boiler controls and smart thermostats can learn how long your system takes to recover and start heating earlier so the home is warm when you wake up.</p><h2>Humidity, drafts and comfort</h2><p>Two homes at the same thermostat setting can feel very different. Drafts from leaky windows and doors make rooms feel colder than the thermostat reads, and very dry air makes skin feel cooler. Sealing drafts, adding insulation and maintaining balanced humidity can let you lower the thermostat a degree or two without feeling it, which adds up to real savings over a long Chicago winter.</p><h2>A sample schedule that works</h2><p>Many families find this pattern comfortable and efficient: about 68 to 70 degrees from early morning until bedtime when people are home, a few degrees cooler overnight and a few degrees cooler during the workday when the house is empty. Heat pump homes should use smaller setbacks of two or three degrees to avoid triggering backup heat, while furnace homes can usually handle larger ones. Adjust based on your home&#x27;s age, insulation and the people living in it.</p><h2>Comfort for working from home</h2><p>If you work from home, heat the rooms you use during the day and consider a slightly lower temperature in unused areas, especially with zoning or radiator valves. A space-specific approach can be more comfortable and efficient than heating the whole house for one home office.</p><h2>Finding your household&#x27;s sweet spot</h2><p>There&#x27;s no single perfect temperature for every household. Try your schedule for a week, notice how everyone feels and adjust a degree at a time. Combined with balanced humidity, sealed drafts and a well-maintained heating system, most Chicago families find a setting that keeps everyone comfortable while keeping winter gas bills under control.</p>
