<h2>Why high-efficiency furnaces make water</h2><p>High-efficiency condensing furnaces cool exhaust gases so much that water vapor condenses into liquid. A typical furnace can produce several gallons of water on a cold day. That water drains through a trap and hose to a floor drain or condensate pump.</p><h2>How a frozen line shuts the furnace down</h2><p>If the drain line freezes or clogs, water backs up into the trap and collector box. A pressure switch senses the problem and stops the furnace from running. The furnace may try to start repeatedly and show a pressure switch fault code.</p><h2>Where freezing happens</h2><p>Lines that run through unheated garages, crawl spaces, exterior walls or near basement windows are most at risk. Condensate pumps that discharge outdoors can also freeze at the outlet during Chicago&#x27;s sub-zero nights.</p><h2>Signs your condensate line is the problem</h2><p>Water around the furnace, a gurgling sound, a pressure switch error code or a furnace that stops during a cold snap after running fine are all common signs.</p><h2>How we fix it</h2><p>We thaw and clear the line, clean the trap, check the condensate pump and test the pressure switch. Then we look at why it froze: we may reroute the drain to a warmer path, insulate it, add heat tape or relocate a pump discharge so the problem doesn&#x27;t return.</p><h2>Preventing it</h2><p>Make sure drain lines don&#x27;t run through cold spaces, keep basement windows near the furnace closed in winter and have your furnace&#x27;s condensate system cleaned during your yearly tune-up.</p><h2>Why this is so common in Chicago</h2><p>Chicago&#x27;s sub-zero nights are exactly the conditions that freeze condensate lines. High-efficiency furnaces are everywhere now, and many were installed with drains that run along cold exterior walls, through unheated garages or out to the yard. When the temperature drops well below zero for several days, those lines freeze, and the furnace shuts down when you need it most. We see a surge of these calls every time a cold snap hits.</p><h2>Condensate pumps and their weak points</h2><p>Basements without a floor drain near the furnace rely on condensate pumps. These small pumps have float switches and check valves that can stick, and their discharge tubing can clog with debris. Some pumps discharge to the outdoors, where the end of the tube can freeze. We check pump operation, clean the reservoir and reroute discharge lines to a safe, freeze-protected location when possible.</p><h2>Vent pipe icing</h2><p>High-efficiency furnaces also vent through PVC pipes that can ice up in extreme cold, especially if the vent terminal is close to the ground or in a spot where snow drifts. An iced vent trips the pressure switch just like a frozen drain. Proper vent termination height and location help prevent it.</p><h2>Prevention during your tune-up</h2><p>During every fall tune-up we flush the condensate trap and drain, test the pump and look at where lines and vents run. If something is likely to freeze, we&#x27;ll recommend a fix before winter arrives rather than after your furnace stops.</p><h2>Error codes to watch for</h2><p>Many high-efficiency furnaces flash a code indicating a pressure switch problem when the condensate system backs up. If you see a blinking light and water around the furnace on a very cold day, the drain is the first suspect. Note the code and share it when you call.</p><h2>A quick fix vs. a permanent one</h2><p>Thawing a frozen line gets the furnace running again, but if the line runs through a cold space it will freeze again during the next cold snap. A permanent fix involves rerouting, insulating or heat-taping the line.</p><h2>Where your condensate should go</h2><p>Ideally, furnace condensate drains by gravity to a floor drain or laundry standpipe inside the heated part of the basement. When that isn&#x27;t possible, a condensate pump lifts the water to a suitable drain. Discharging condensate outdoors in Chicago is risky because the end of the line can freeze. Moving the discharge to an indoor drain is often the best long-term fix.</p><h2>The bottom line</h2><p>A frozen condensate line is one of the most common reasons high-efficiency furnaces stop during Chicago cold snaps, but it&#x27;s also one of the most preventable. Proper drain routing, a working condensate pump with an indoor discharge and a yearly cleaning of the trap and lines keep water flowing all winter. If your furnace has shut down with water around it, call us and we&#x27;ll get it running and make sure it doesn&#x27;t happen again.</p>
