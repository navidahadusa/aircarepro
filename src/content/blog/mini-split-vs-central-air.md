<h2>When central air makes sense</h2><p>If your home already has a forced-air furnace and ductwork in good shape, central air is usually the simplest and most cost-effective option. One system cools the whole home through existing vents.</p><h2>When mini-splits make sense</h2><p>For homes heated by radiators, attics, additions and finished basements, mini-splits avoid the cost and disruption of adding ducts. They&#x27;re also great for rooms that central air can&#x27;t keep comfortable.</p><h2>Comfort and control</h2><p>Mini-splits let you set each room independently. Central air uses one thermostat, unless you add zoning.</p><h2>Efficiency</h2><p>Mini-splits avoid duct losses and use variable-speed compressors. Central systems can also be very efficient, especially with good ductwork and variable-speed equipment.</p><h2>Appearance</h2><p>Central air hides behind vents. Mini-splits have visible indoor units, though ceiling cassettes and slim ducted units offer a cleaner look.</p><h2>Heating too</h2><p>Mini-splits are heat pumps, so they also heat. Central air only cools unless you choose a heat pump.</p><h2>Cost factors for central air</h2><p>For homes with existing ductwork, central air installation involves the outdoor unit, an indoor coil on top of your furnace, a line set, electrical work and drainage. If the ducts are in good shape and properly sized, the cost is often lower than a whole-home ductless system. If the ductwork needs major changes, the cost and disruption increase.</p><h2>Cost factors for mini-splits</h2><p>Mini-split costs depend on the number of indoor units, their style and the length and routing of line sets. A single-zone system for one room is relatively simple. A multi-zone system for an entire home costs more but avoids the expense of installing ductwork in a home that doesn&#x27;t have it.</p><h2>Older Chicago homes</h2><p>In Chicago, many homes have boilers and radiators with no ductwork at all. For these homes, mini-splits are often the clear winner: efficient cooling, supplemental heat and minimal disruption to plaster walls and historic details.</p><h2>Two-story comfort</h2><p>In two-story homes with central air, upstairs rooms often run hot. Adding a mini-split upstairs, or improving ductwork and zoning, can solve the problem. Sometimes a combination of central air and a ductless unit is the most effective solution.</p><h2>Maintenance differences</h2><p>Central systems need filter changes and yearly tune-ups. Mini-splits need indoor filter cleaning every few weeks during heavy use and periodic professional deep cleaning of indoor units. Both benefit from regular maintenance.</p><h2>Real example: Chicago two-flat</h2><p>In a typical Chicago two-flat heated by a boiler, adding central air would mean installing ductwork through finished plaster walls and ceilings, an expensive and disruptive project. A multi-zone mini-split system can cool bedrooms and living areas in each unit with small wall penetrations, often in a day or two per unit, while providing extra heat in spring and fall.</p><h2>Real example: suburban two-story</h2><p>In a suburban two-story home with a furnace and ductwork, central air is usually the simplest choice. If the upstairs runs hot, adding a ductless unit to the hottest bedroom or improving ductwork can fix it.</p><h2>Resale considerations</h2><p>Both systems add value. Buyers in older neighborhoods increasingly expect air conditioning, and ductless systems are widely accepted in homes without ducts.</p><h2>Hybrid approaches</h2><p>Some homes get the best results from combining approaches. For example, central air can handle the main floors through existing ducts while a single-zone mini-split cools a finished attic or sunroom. In boiler homes, a multi-zone mini-split might serve bedrooms while a high-velocity small-duct system serves living areas. There isn&#x27;t one right answer for every home, which is why we evaluate each house individually before recommending a system.</p><h2>Efficiency ratings to compare</h2><p>Both systems are rated by SEER2 for cooling, and heat pumps and mini-splits also have heating ratings. Comparing these ratings, along with installation quality, helps you understand long-term operating costs.</p><h2>Questions to ask before deciding</h2><p>Does your home have ductwork in good condition? Which rooms are uncomfortable now? Do you need heating as well as cooling? How important is the look of indoor units? What&#x27;s your budget, and how long do you plan to stay? Your answers will point toward central air, mini-splits or a combination.</p><h2>Get an expert opinion</h2><p>We install both, so we have no reason to push one over the other. We&#x27;ll recommend what will actually work best in your home.</p>
