<h2>What zoning is</h2><p>A zoned system divides your home into areas, each with its own thermostat. Motorized dampers in the ductwork open and close to send heated or cooled air only where it&#x27;s needed.</p><h2>How it works</h2><p>A zone control panel receives calls from each thermostat, opens the right dampers and turns on the furnace or AC. When a zone is satisfied, its damper closes.</p><h2>Who benefits most</h2><p>Two-story homes, homes with finished basements, large open layouts and homes with rooms that get lots of sun benefit most from zoning.</p><h2>Equipment matters</h2><p>Zoning works best with two-stage or variable-speed equipment that can reduce output when only one zone is calling. Bypass or static pressure controls may be needed on single-stage systems.</p><h2>Ductwork considerations</h2><p>Ducts must be sized and arranged so each zone can get enough air. We evaluate ductwork before recommending zoning.</p><h2>Alternatives</h2><p>Sometimes balancing dampers, added returns or a ductless unit solve the problem more simply. We&#x27;ll recommend what fits.</p><h2>Signs your home could benefit from zoning</h2><p>Upstairs bedrooms that are too hot in summer while the main floor is cold, a finished basement that&#x27;s always chilly, rooms with big south or west windows that overheat in the afternoon and constant thermostat battles between family members are all signs that zoning could help. These problems are common in two-story Chicago-area homes and larger suburban houses.</p><h2>Zone dampers explained</h2><p>Zone dampers are motorized plates installed in the ductwork. When a zone needs heating or cooling, its damper opens; when it&#x27;s satisfied, the damper closes. Dampers can be installed in main trunk lines or branch ducts depending on the layout. Proper placement is key to good performance.</p><h2>Zone control panels</h2><p>The zone panel is the brain of the system. It receives signals from each thermostat, controls the dampers and tells the furnace, air conditioner or heat pump when to run and at what stage. Panels like the one we wire in many homes support multiple zones, multi-stage equipment and heat pumps.</p><h2>Static pressure and bypass</h2><p>When only one small zone is calling, the system can push more air than that zone&#x27;s ducts can handle, raising static pressure and noise. Variable-speed equipment, careful zone sizing and, in some cases, bypass or dump zones manage this. We design zoning systems to avoid these problems.</p><h2>Is zoning right for your home?</h2><p>Zoning works well when ductwork can be divided sensibly and equipment can adjust its output. In some homes, a ductless unit for a problem area is simpler. We&#x27;ll evaluate your home and recommend the best solution.</p><h2>Zoning and smart thermostats</h2><p>Each zone can have its own smart thermostat with schedules, so bedrooms can be cooler at night while living areas stay comfortable. Some systems support remote sensors that improve comfort further.</p><h2>Zoning in boiler homes</h2><p>Hot water boiler systems are naturally easy to zone with zone valves or multiple circulators. Many Chicago boiler homes already have two or more zones, each with its own thermostat.</p><h2>Maintenance</h2><p>Zone dampers and panels need occasional checks to make sure dampers move freely and thermostats communicate properly. We include zoning checks in tune-ups for zoned homes.</p><h2>Real example: two-story suburban home</h2><p>In a typical two-story suburban home with one furnace and one thermostat on the first floor, the upstairs is often several degrees warmer in summer and the basement several degrees cooler in winter. Adding a two-zone system with dampers for the upstairs and downstairs, paired with a variable-speed furnace and AC, lets each floor reach its own set temperature. Homeowners often notice the difference the first night, when the bedrooms finally cool down.</p><h2>Costs to consider</h2><p>Zoning cost depends on the number of zones, damper locations, duct access, the zone panel and thermostats. Adding zoning during a new system installation is often the most efficient time.</p><h2>Zoning vs. a second system</h2><p>In very large homes, a second furnace and air conditioner for the upper floor may be a better solution than zoning a single system. Two systems provide redundancy and can be sized for each floor&#x27;s needs. In typical two-story homes, zoning with variable-speed equipment is often more cost-effective. We&#x27;ll compare both options for your home.</p><h2>The bottom line</h2><p>Zoning can turn a home with constant hot and cold spots into one where every area is comfortable. Proper design is essential, so work with a contractor who evaluates your ductwork and equipment first.</p>
