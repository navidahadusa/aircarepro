<h2>Signs of air in the system</h2><p>Gurgling or trickling sounds in pipes and radiators, radiators that are hot at the bottom but cool at the top and upper floors that don&#x27;t heat well are classic signs of trapped air in a hot water system.</p><h2>Where air comes from</h2><p>Fresh water added to the system carries dissolved air that&#x27;s released as the water heats. Air can also enter through small leaks, low system pressure or during repairs when the system is drained.</p><h2>Bleeding radiators</h2><p>With the system running, open each radiator&#x27;s bleed valve with a radiator key until water appears, then close it. Start at the lowest floor and work up. Check boiler pressure afterward, because bleeding can lower it.</p><h2>Automatic air vents and air scoops</h2><p>Many systems have an air scoop near the boiler with an automatic air vent on top. These vents release air automatically, but over time they can corrode, leak or stick closed. A corroded vent should be replaced before it leaks or stops working.</p><h2>When air keeps coming back</h2><p>If you have to bleed radiators constantly, there may be a leak, a failed fill valve or a system design issue. A technician can find the cause and fix it permanently.</p><h2>Professional purging</h2><p>Sometimes air is trapped in places bleeding can&#x27;t reach. Technicians can purge the system using valves near the boiler to push air out of the piping.</p><h2>Why upper floors suffer first</h2><p>Air rises to the highest points in a hot water system, so top-floor radiators and the upper parts of radiators are where it collects. In two-flats and three-flats, the top unit often notices the problem first, with cool radiators and gurgling sounds. Bleeding those radiators, starting from the lowest floor and working up, is usually the first step.</p><h2>Low pressure and air go together</h2><p>When system pressure is too low, water may not reach the top of the system, leaving air pockets in upper radiators and piping. Checking and correcting system pressure is just as important as bleeding radiators. If pressure keeps dropping, there&#x27;s likely a leak to find.</p><h2>Microbubble air separators</h2><p>Some systems benefit from a modern air separator that removes tiny bubbles from the water as it circulates. These devices can dramatically reduce recurring air problems, especially in larger or older systems.</p><h2>The role of corroded vents</h2><p>Automatic air vents on air scoops can corrode, especially in damp basements. A corroded vent may stick closed, letting air build up, or stick open and leak water. Replacing a worn vent is an inexpensive repair that prevents bigger problems.</p><h2>Bleeding radiators step by step</h2><p>Have a towel and small container ready. With the system running, place the radiator key in the bleed valve near the top of the radiator and turn it slowly counterclockwise. You&#x27;ll hear hissing as air escapes. When a steady stream of water appears, close the valve. Repeat for each radiator, then check the boiler pressure gauge and call us if pressure has dropped below the normal range.</p><h2>Baseboard systems</h2><p>Hot water baseboard systems may have bleed valves at the end of runs or rely on purge valves near the boiler. Purging these systems properly often requires a technician.</p><h2>When to call a professional</h2><p>If bleeding radiators doesn&#x27;t solve cold spots, if you hear constant gurgling, if pressure keeps dropping or if you have a baseboard system without bleed valves, call a technician. We can purge air from the system, check the fill valve and expansion tank, replace failing air vents and look for leaks that keep letting air in. In larger buildings, adding a modern air separator can dramatically reduce recurring air problems and protect pumps from wear.</p><h2>Air and corrosion</h2><p>Oxygen dissolved in system water contributes to corrosion inside boilers, pipes and radiators over time. Keeping the system tight, without constant fresh water being added, reduces that corrosion. That&#x27;s another reason to fix leaks and failing vents promptly rather than repeatedly bleeding and refilling.</p><h2>The bottom line</h2><p>Occasional air is normal, especially at the start of the heating season. Persistent air, constant bleeding or dropping pressure point to a problem worth fixing. We&#x27;ll find and fix the cause so your radiators stay warm and quiet.</p>
