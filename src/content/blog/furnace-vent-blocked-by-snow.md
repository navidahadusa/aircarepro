A Chicago snowstorm doesn't only bury the car. If your furnace vents through white PVC pipes on the side of the house, a drift against that wall can stop the heat in the middle of the night. It happens most after heavy, wet snow or a stretch of blowing snow, when drifts pile up against the side of a bungalow or along a narrow gangway, the parts of the yard nobody shovels.

The good news: when snow is the only cause, a homeowner can usually fix it in a few minutes.

## Which furnaces have this problem

High-efficiency furnaces (the condensing kind, usually installed in the last 20 to 25 years) cool their exhaust so much that it can vent through plastic pipe instead of a chimney. The pipes usually leave the house through a side wall or the rim joist, often just above the ground or a little higher.

You'll see one of two setups outside:

- **Two pipes:** one for exhaust, one bringing in outside air for combustion. Both have to stay clear.
- **One pipe, or a single round "concentric" cap:** exhaust only, or exhaust and intake combined in one fitting.

An older furnace that vents into a brick chimney through a metal flue doesn't have this issue, though a chimney can have problems of its own. If you're not sure what you have, look at the furnace: white PVC pipes running out of it toward an outside wall mean it's the high-efficiency type.

## Why snow shuts the furnace off

Before the burner lights, a small fan called the inducer pushes air through the vent. A pressure switch checks that the air is actually moving. If snow or ice blocks the pipe, the switch never closes and the furnace won't light. This is a deliberate safety stop that keeps exhaust from backing up into the house.

What you might notice:

- The furnace starts, you hear the inducer fan hum, then it stops without lighting.
- It cycles on and off over and over.
- A blinking light or code on the control board (often listed on a sticker inside the panel door as a pressure switch fault).
- The house slowly cools even though the thermostat is calling for heat.

Ice can do the same thing without any drift. In very cold weather, moisture in the exhaust can freeze at the end of the pipe and slowly close it off. Water dripping from the roof onto the pipe and refreezing is another common cause.

## How to clear the vents safely

1. **Find the pipes** on the outside wall closest to the furnace. If snow has covered them completely, dig carefully by hand or with a soft brush until you can see the openings.
2. **Clear at least a foot around them** so the next gust doesn't fill them right back in. Leave space below the pipes too.
3. **Check the openings for ice.** Knock off loose ice gently with a gloved hand. Don't chip at the pipe with a shovel, ice scraper or screwdriver: PVC gets brittle in the cold and cracks easily.
4. **Don't push anything into the pipe** and don't use a heat gun or open flame on it.
5. **Restart the furnace.** Set the thermostat to Off for a few minutes, then back to Heat. If it doesn't start, turn the furnace off at its service switch (it looks like a light switch, often near the furnace or at the top of the basement stairs) for about a minute and turn it back on.

Give it a few minutes. You should hear the inducer, then the burner lighting, then the blower.

While you're outside, look at the other things that vent through that wall: the dryer vent and, in some homes, a power-vented water heater. Your gas meter needs attention too. Brush snow off it gently and keep ice from building up on it and its regulator, which has a small vent of its own.

## When to stop and call

Call for service if:

- The furnace still won't light after the vents are clear and you've restarted it once or twice.
- A vent pipe is cracked, loose, sagging or pulled away from the wall.
- You see ice building up inside the pipe, or water pooling at the furnace (a frozen or blocked condensate drain can cause the same kind of shutdown; see [frozen furnace condensate lines](/frozen-furnace-condensate/)).
- The vents keep getting buried, storm after storm.

Don't keep pressing reset or switching the furnace on and off. If it keeps locking out, something else is going on. Our [furnace won't turn on](/furnace-wont-turn-on/) guide covers the other common causes.

**If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds**, or anyone at home has a headache, dizziness or nausea that eases outdoors, get everyone outside and call 911 first. If you smell gas, leave and call your gas utility from outside. Read more about [carbon monoxide safety](/carbon-monoxide-safety/).

## Before the next storm

- Find your vent pipes now, while you can see them, so you know where to dig in a blizzard.
- Keep snow from piling against that wall when you shovel or plow.
- Don't stack firewood, bins or patio furniture in front of the pipes.
- Test your CO alarms and replace any that are past their expiration date.
- A fall [furnace tune-up](/furnace-tune-up/) includes a look at the vents, the pressure switch and the condensate drain.
