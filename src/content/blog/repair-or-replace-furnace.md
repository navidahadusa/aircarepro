<h2>Age matters</h2><p>Most gas furnaces last roughly 15 to 20 years. A furnace in that range that needs a major repair is often a candidate for replacement, while a younger furnace is usually worth fixing.</p><h2>Look at the repair history</h2><p>One repair is normal. Several repairs in a couple of years suggest the furnace is wearing out, and the money may be better spent on a new system.</p><h2>Safety comes first</h2><p>A cracked heat exchanger or venting problem can be a safety hazard. In those cases, replacement is often the right call.</p><h2>Efficiency and comfort</h2><p>Older furnaces may be around 80 percent efficient or less, while modern high-efficiency furnaces reach 95 percent or more. New variable-speed furnaces also run quieter and keep temperatures more even.</p><h2>Get honest options</h2><p>A good technician will show you the repair cost and replacement options side by side and let you decide. That&#x27;s how we work at Chicago Aircare Pro, with free estimates on new installations and financing available.</p><h2>Think about your plans</h2><p>If you plan to stay in your home for many years, a new high-efficiency furnace pays you back through lower gas bills and fewer repairs. If you may move soon, a reasonable repair might make more sense, although a new furnace can also be a selling point.</p><h2>Consider your air conditioner too</h2><p>Furnaces and air conditioners share the blower and ductwork. If both are aging, replacing them together can improve efficiency and compatibility and may cost less than replacing them separately a few years apart.</p><h2>Watch for warning signs</h2><p>Rising gas bills, uneven heat, more dust, strange noises, a yellow burner flame or soot around the furnace are signs that it&#x27;s struggling. Don&#x27;t ignore them, especially any sign of a combustion or venting problem.</p><h2>The 50 percent rule of thumb</h2><p>A common guideline is to consider replacement when a repair would cost more than half the price of a new furnace, especially if the furnace is past the middle of its expected life. It&#x27;s not a hard rule, but it&#x27;s a useful starting point for the conversation.</p><h2>What a new furnace can improve</h2><p>Beyond efficiency, new furnaces can be quieter, keep temperatures more even and work better with humidifiers and air cleaners. Variable-speed blowers and two-stage burners run longer at lower output, reducing hot and cold swings that older single-stage furnaces create.</p><h2>Financing and rebates</h2><p>If replacement makes sense but the timing is tough, financing can spread the cost over time. Utility rebates on high-efficiency equipment may also reduce the price. We&#x27;ll help you look at both.</p><h2>Questions to ask your technician</h2><p>When you&#x27;re deciding between repair and replacement, ask your technician a few direct questions. How old is the furnace and what condition is the heat exchanger in? What failed and why? Is this part likely to be the first of several failures? How efficient is the current furnace compared with a new one? What would a properly sized replacement look like for my home? A good technician will answer clearly and show you what they found instead of pushing you toward one answer. At Chicago Aircare Pro, we put both options side by side so you can decide with full information.</p><h2>Don&#x27;t forget the ductwork</h2><p>A new furnace is only as good as the ductwork it connects to. If your home has always had hot and cold rooms, noisy vents or a basement that&#x27;s much warmer than the upstairs, the problem may be duct design rather than the furnace itself. When we quote a replacement, we check static pressure and return air and recommend improvements where they&#x27;ll make a real difference. Sometimes a modest duct fix combined with a properly sized furnace solves comfort problems that homeowners have lived with for decades.</p><h2>Timing your replacement</h2><p>The best time to replace a furnace is before it fails completely. Late summer and early fall give you time to compare options, check rebates and schedule installation without the pressure of a cold house. If your furnace is aging and you&#x27;ve had repairs in the last couple of winters, consider getting a replacement estimate during your fall tune-up. That way, if a major repair is needed in January, you already know your options and can make a quick decision.</p><h2>A simple way to compare</h2><p>Write down the repair quote, the furnace&#x27;s age and how many repairs it&#x27;s needed in the last three years. Then compare that against a replacement estimate and the potential gas savings of a high-efficiency model. Seeing the numbers side by side usually makes the decision clear.</p><h2>Our promise on recommendations</h2><p>We never recommend replacing a furnace that can be reasonably repaired. If repair makes sense, we&#x27;ll say so plainly. If replacement is the smarter choice, we&#x27;ll explain exactly why and give you options at several price points, with financing available.</p>
