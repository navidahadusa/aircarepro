<h2>Check the thermostat first</h2><p>Make sure the thermostat is set to Heat, not Cool, and that the fan is set to Auto instead of On. When the fan is set to On, it runs constantly and blows room-temperature air between heating cycles, which feels cold.</p><h2>Look at the filter</h2><p>A badly clogged filter restricts airflow. The furnace can overheat and shut its burners off as a safety measure while the blower keeps running. Replace the filter and give the furnace a few minutes to reset.</p><h2>High-efficiency furnace drain problems</h2><p>High-efficiency furnaces make water. If the condensate drain or trap clogs, or the drain line freezes, a safety switch stops the burners. This is one of the most common winter calls we get in Chicago.</p><h2>Ignition and flame sensor problems</h2><p>If the igniter is cracked or the flame sensor is dirty, the furnace will try to light, fail and lock out. You may see a blinking light code on the control board. A technician can clean or replace these parts quickly.</p><h2>When to call</h2><p>If the basics don&#x27;t fix it, or you smell gas, call a professional. Never try to bypass safety switches. Chicago Aircare Pro offers 24/7 emergency heating service across Chicago and the north suburbs.</p><h2>Check the furnace switch and breaker</h2><p>Furnaces have a power switch that looks like a light switch, usually on or near the unit. Make sure it&#x27;s on and the breaker hasn&#x27;t tripped. Also check that the furnace door panel is fully closed, because a safety switch keeps the furnace off when the door is open.</p><h2>Ductwork leaks can feel like cold air</h2><p>If the furnace is producing heat but some vents blow cool air, leaky or disconnected ducts in an unheated space may be losing heat before it reaches the room. This is common in attics and crawl spaces and is worth having inspected.</p><h2>What a technician will do</h2><p>A technician reads the furnace&#x27;s fault codes, tests the igniter, flame sensor, pressure switches and limit switches, checks gas pressure and confirms safe venting. Most causes of cold air are fixed on the first visit.</p><h2>Is it normal for the furnace to blow cool air at first?</h2><p>Yes. When a cycle starts, the burners light first and the blower waits a short time before turning on, so the heat exchanger can warm up. At the end of a cycle, the blower keeps running to pull remaining heat out. During those few seconds, air from the vents can feel cool, especially if ducts run through a cold basement or attic. If the air stays cool for the whole cycle, that&#x27;s a problem.</p><h2>Heat pump homes are different</h2><p>If you have a heat pump rather than a gas furnace, the air coming from your vents will feel cooler than furnace air, typically around 90 to 100 degrees rather than 120 or more. That&#x27;s normal. But if a heat pump blows truly cold air, it may be stuck in defrost, have a reversing valve problem or be low on refrigerant.</p><h2>Preventing the problem</h2><p>Most cold-air calls could be avoided with a yearly furnace tune-up. Cleaning the flame sensor, checking the igniter, clearing the condensate drain and replacing the filter take care of the most common causes before winter. Members of our maintenance plan get this service every fall, plus priority scheduling if anything goes wrong.</p><h2>A Chicago example</h2><p>On a typical January service call, a homeowner tells us the furnace runs but the house keeps getting colder. When we arrive, the furnace is trying to light every few minutes, the burners stay on for three or four seconds and then shut off, and the blower keeps pushing room-temperature air through the vents. Nine times out of ten, the flame sensor is coated and the control board can&#x27;t confirm there&#x27;s a flame. A few minutes of cleaning and a full test cycle later, the house is warming up again. It&#x27;s a simple fix, but it&#x27;s also one that a yearly tune-up would have caught before the cold snap.</p><h2>Older furnaces and cold air</h2><p>If your furnace is more than 15 years old and you&#x27;re getting cold air more often, it may be a sign of broader wear. Older igniters, gas valves and limit switches become less reliable, and heat exchangers can develop problems that cause the furnace to overheat and shut the burners off. When cold-air complaints become a pattern, it&#x27;s worth having a technician assess the furnace&#x27;s overall condition and give you a straight opinion on whether repair or replacement makes more sense for your home and budget.</p><h2>The bottom line</h2><p>Cold air from a furnace almost always has a specific, fixable cause. Start with the simple checks, and if the problem continues, call a technician who will diagnose it properly rather than guessing. A warm house is usually just one repair away.</p>
