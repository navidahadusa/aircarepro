<p>A boiler not heating is a different problem in a Chicago greystone than in a bungalow. Many two-flats and older homes here still run on steam, while most bungalows and newer installs use hot water pumped to radiators or baseboard. The first step is to know which one you have, because the checks and the likely causes are different. If you are not sure, our guide to <a href="/boiler-vs-furnace/">boilers and furnaces</a> explains the basics. A pressure gauge in pounds per square inch (psi) and a circulator pump usually mean hot water; a glass tube showing the water level (a sight glass) usually means steam.</p>

<h2>Safe checks before you call</h2>
<p>These checks are safe for most homeowners and often bring the heat back:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Thermostat:</strong> set to Heat, a few degrees above room temperature, with fresh batteries. In zoned homes each thermostat controls its own zone, so one cold area may mean one thermostat isn't calling for heat.</li>
<li><strong>Power:</strong> the boiler's service switch (often at the top of the basement stairs, looking like a light switch) and the breaker.</li>
<li><strong>Hot-water boilers:</strong> look at the pressure gauge. If the pressure is far below where it normally sits, the system may be losing water. See <a href="/boiler-pressure-guide/">our boiler pressure guide</a> for what the reading means.</li>
<li><strong>Steam boilers:</strong> look at the sight glass. The water should be visible partway up the glass. If it is empty, turn the boiler off and let it cool. Don't add cold water to a hot, dry boiler: it can crack the cast iron.</li>
<li><strong>Reset:</strong> if the burner control has a reset button and a lockout light, press it once. If it locks out again, stop there.</li>
<li><strong>Radiators:</strong> make sure valves are open and nothing is blocking them. On hot-water systems, bleeding air from upstairs radiators can bring them back (see <a href="/air-in-boiler-system/">air in a boiler system</a>).</li>
</ul>

<h2>Boiler won't fire at all</h2>
<p>If the boiler is silent with power on and a call for heat, the burner isn't lighting. Common causes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ignition problems.</strong> Older boilers with a standing pilot rely on a thermocouple or thermopile to keep the gas valve open. When those weaken, the pilot goes out. Newer boilers use an electronic igniter and flame sensor that can fail or get dirty. Relighting a pilot may get you through a night, but if it keeps going out, the part needs to be replaced. If you smell gas, don't try to relight it.</li>
<li><strong>Low-water cut-off.</strong> This safety device shuts the burner down when the water level drops too low. It is doing its job: the real question is why the water is low (a leak, a failed automatic feeder or, on steam, water lost as steam over time).</li>
<li><strong>Pressure and limit controls.</strong> On steam boilers a pressuretrol stops the burner at its set pressure; a clogged pigtail can make it shut off too early. On hot-water boilers the aquastat or high limit can fail.</li>
<li><strong>Control board, relays and wiring.</strong> Modern boilers have boards, relays and transformers that can fail. Many show fault codes that point a technician to the right component.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Boiler runs but radiators stay cold</h2>
<p>If the boiler is hot but the house isn't, heat is being made and not delivered.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Circulator pump (hot-water).</strong> The pump moves hot water from the boiler to the radiators. When its motor, bearings or coupling fail, the boiler gets hot and the radiators stay cold. Sometimes the pump hums without spinning; sometimes it is silent.</li>
<li><strong>Zone valves (hot-water).</strong> A zone valve with a failed motor or end switch can leave one zone cold, or keep the boiler from firing for that zone.</li>
<li><strong>Air in the system (hot-water).</strong> Trapped air collects at high points and blocks flow, which is why the top floor of a two-flat often goes cold first.</li>
<li><strong>Vents and water level (steam).</strong> Steam can't enter a radiator until air leaves it. Failed main vents in the basement or stuck radiator vents leave radiators cold or slow to heat. Too much water in the boiler can cause noisy, wet steam (see <a href="/steam-heat-banging-pipes/">banging steam pipes</a>).</li>
</ul>

<h2>Boiler heat not keeping up in a cold snap</h2>
<p>When lake-effect cold sets in and nights drop well below zero, every weakness shows. A boiler that runs nonstop while rooms fall behind may have dirty burners, a scaled or sooted heat exchanger, a weak circulator, or radiators that were removed or covered during past renovations. Some boilers were simply sized for a different house: rooms added, windows changed, a two-flat turned into one home. A technician can measure how the system is performing and tell you whether it needs a repair, a cleaning or, eventually, a properly sized replacement.</p>

<h2>Only one floor or one zone is cold</h2>
<p>Uneven heat usually points to one part of the system. In a zoned hot-water system, check the thermostat for that zone first, then suspect its zone valve or circulator. In shared-boiler two-flats, the upper unit is often the first to lose heat when air builds up or the pressure is low. On steam, one cold radiator usually means a failed radiator vent or a pipe that has lost its pitch, so condensate pools and blocks the steam.</p>

<p>If you smell gas, leave the building and call your gas utility from outside. If your carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone out and call 911. No heat in freezing weather is an emergency too: pipes in exterior walls and unheated basements can freeze.
