<h2>Reading your boiler gauge</h2><p>Most hot water boilers have a combination gauge showing pressure in psi and temperature. On many residential systems, pressure is around 12 to 15 psi when the system is cold and rises somewhat as the water heats. The right range depends on your building&#x27;s height and system design, so your technician can confirm what&#x27;s normal for you.</p><h2>Why pressure drops</h2><p>Low pressure can come from a leak in piping or radiators, a leaking relief valve, air being vented from the system or a fill valve that isn&#x27;t working. Low pressure can cause cold radiators on upper floors and may trigger the boiler&#x27;s low water cut-off.</p><h2>Why pressure climbs too high</h2><p>High pressure is often caused by a waterlogged expansion tank that can no longer absorb expansion, or a fill valve that lets in too much water. If pressure reaches the relief valve setting, the relief valve will discharge water to protect the boiler.</p><h2>The expansion tank&#x27;s role</h2><p>Water expands as it heats. The expansion tank has an air cushion that absorbs that expansion. When the tank&#x27;s bladder fails or loses its air charge, pressure swings widely. Replacing or recharging the tank fixes it.</p><h2>What you can do</h2><p>Note the pressure when the system is cold and when it&#x27;s hot. If you see water dripping from the relief valve, pressure near the relief setting, or pressure dropping repeatedly, call for service. Don&#x27;t try to adjust the fill valve or relief valve yourself.</p><h2>Steam boilers are different</h2><p>Steam boilers operate at very low pressure, often just a fraction of a psi to a couple psi. If your steam boiler runs at higher pressure, banging pipes and uneven heat can result. A technician can adjust the pressure control.</p><h2>Taller buildings need more pressure</h2><p>The water in a hot water system has to be pushed up to the highest radiator. A three-flat needs more system pressure than a single-story home to keep the top floor radiators full. That&#x27;s why there isn&#x27;t one correct number for every building. Your technician can calculate the right fill pressure based on the height of your system and set the fill valve accordingly.</p><h2>The relief valve is a safety device</h2><p>Every boiler has a pressure relief valve, usually set around 30 psi on residential systems. If pressure reaches that point, the valve opens and discharges water to protect the boiler. A relief valve that drips or discharges isn&#x27;t the problem itself; it&#x27;s a symptom of pressure climbing too high, usually from a failed expansion tank or fill valve. Never cap or plug a relief valve.</p><h2>Checking your expansion tank</h2><p>Many modern systems use a bladder-type expansion tank, often a gray or blue tank hanging near the boiler. Over time, the bladder can fail or the air charge can leak out. A technician can check the tank&#x27;s air pressure and replace it if needed. Older systems may use a steel compression tank in the basement ceiling, which can become waterlogged and need draining.</p><h2>When to call us</h2><p>Call for service if your boiler pressure keeps dropping, rises near the relief setting when hot, if water is discharging from the relief valve or if upper-floor radiators won&#x27;t heat. These problems are usually straightforward to fix once diagnosed.</p><h2>Pressure and temperature together</h2><p>Most residential boilers have a combination gauge showing both pressure and temperature. Watching both helps you understand how your system behaves. If pressure climbs sharply as temperature rises, the expansion tank may not be absorbing expansion as it should.</p><h2>Annual boiler service</h2><p>A yearly boiler tune-up includes checking pressure, testing the relief valve, inspecting the expansion tank, checking circulators and controls, cleaning burners and testing combustion and venting.</p><h2>Pressure during the off-season</h2><p>Boiler pressure may drop slightly over the summer as the system cools and sits idle. Check it before the heating season starts. If it&#x27;s well below normal, have a technician check for leaks and refill the system properly.</p><h2>The bottom line</h2><p>Your boiler&#x27;s pressure gauge is a simple but valuable tool. Knowing what&#x27;s normal for your system helps you spot problems early, whether that&#x27;s a slow leak, a failing expansion tank or a fill valve that isn&#x27;t working right. If the numbers look wrong or the relief valve is dripping, call us. Most pressure problems are straightforward to diagnose and fix, and catching them early protects your boiler and keeps your radiators warm all winter.</p>
